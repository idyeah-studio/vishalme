The problem

A missing instrument can stop a surgery.

The coordinator who keeps a da Vinci program stocked is upstream of the surgery itself. Order too little and you risk delaying or complicating a case. Order too much and you burn the budget on instruments that expire against a use limit. High stakes, low visibility, and no room to be casually wrong.

The portal meant to help was built in 2016, and it aged as the programs grew. By the time I arrived, three of four users were dissatisfied, the core functions sat three and four levels deep, and the returns flow went wrong more often than it went right. That was the starting line, not a scoreboard.

This is not a dashboard job. It is upstream of whether a surgery goes smoothly.

The real problem

They did not need more data. They needed the decision.

So I stopped reading the portal and watched the people. Contextual inquiry in the real workflow, then a moderated usability test: a deliberate mix of novice and expert coordinators, eight task scenarios across navigation, ordering, and reading utilization.

The same pattern kept surfacing. Nurses were not short on data; the portal had plenty. Every one of them did the same silent math, scanning columns to turn a spreadsheet of usage into a single answer: do I reorder this, and how much. The decision was in there. It was just buried under the data instead of expressed by it.

Buried under the data, instead of expressed by it.

The principle

Show the decision, not the data.

Once I could name it, the redesign stopped being about tidying screens and became about surfacing a judgment. I threw out the filing cabinet of instruments, systems, and orders in their separate silos, and organized the whole tool around the three things a coordinator actually does.

The bet

I made the riskiest view the primary one.

I moved the treemap to the front and made it the primary ordering view. Cell size encodes quantity and usage, so the reorder signal is not something you calculate. It is something you see. Big cells and small cells tell you where attention is owed before you read a single number.

The risk was real. A treemap has a steeper first day than a spreadsheet, and these coordinators were fluent in order history tables. I was asking experts to trade a familiar tool for an unfamiliar one, betting that day two would beat day one. In testing, nurses read their instrument usage at a glance for the first time, and task completion on ordering rose measurably over the baseline.

The architecture

Organized around the job, not the database.

The old portal was a filing cabinet: instruments here, systems there, orders and returns in their own silos, so one real task meant hopping across four of them. I collapsed it around the coordinator's actual work. Functions once three and four levels deep now sit one step from the door.

Then I made the rest honest. Returns, the workflow that failed most often, was rebuilt with progressive disclosure and inline validation so the correct path is the obvious path. A cost against use scatter lets administrators spot over-ordered instruments on sight, and a utilization summary breaks hundreds of cases and hours across the Xi, Si, and SP systems. And because coordinators move between desktop and tablet across a shift, the tool follows them instead of pinning them to a desk.

Navigation, rebuilt around the job. The information architecture reorganized so the depth did not shrink a little, it collapsed, across every breakpoint and theme.

Utilization at a glance. Cases and hours across systems, with a history calendar for capacity planning.

Cost against use. A scatter that surfaces over-ordered instruments on sight, for the administrators above the coordinators.

Survive contact with reality

A redesign this opinionated only ships if the reasoning is legible.

So I did not hand over a pile of screens and hope. I ran a feasibility study with engineering in parallel with the design, three questions at once: are we process ready, can we get the data, can we build it. Because they were in it from day one, we caught a real constraint early and dropped a feature before it cost a sprint. There were no surprises mid build.

And I scored every feature on a Value, Feasibility, and Priority matrix, each one mapped to the job it served. The bets were legible. Anyone could see why the treemap came before the nice to haves, and that artifact became the roadmap product and engineering actually built from.

The test plan. A mix of novice and expert coordinators, eight task scenarios, sixty minute sessions. Findings validated, not asserted.

The roadmap. Value against feasibility against priority, every feature scored, so the build order was a shared, legible argument.
