Intuitive Surgical // da Vinci

A missing instrument can stop a surgery. The software meant to prevent that had quietly stopped working.

Three out of four nurses running da Vinci robotic programs were fighting the tool meant to help them order surgical instruments. I was brought in to fix a portal. What I found was a decision the software was hiding.

Role
Lead Product / UX Designer
Scope
Contextual research, usability testing, IA, novel data visualization, responsive design, production handoff
Client
Intuitive Surgical
Shipped · Intuitive
Utilization at a glance: cases, hours, and fleet status for a da Vinci program, in one view.
01 // The stakes

The people no one watches, doing the job no one sees

Walk into an operating room that runs da Vinci robots and you will not notice the nurse who makes the whole thing possible. The surgeon has the spotlight. The robotic coordinator, the ROCO, has the inventory. She is the reason the right instrument is in the right tray at the right minute, and the reason a four-arm robot does not stall halfway through a procedure waiting on a part that was never ordered.

This is not a dashboard job. Instrument availability is upstream of surgical outcomes. Order too little and you risk delaying or complicating a case. Order too much and you burn a budget on instruments that expire against a use limit. The margin for being wrong is thin, and the person holding that margin is doing it between cases, on her feet, on whatever screen is closest.

That is the world the software lived in. High stakes, low visibility, no room to be casually wrong.

This is not a dashboard job. It is upstream of whether a surgery goes smoothly.
02 // The compounding failure

A tool built for 2016, aging inside a job that kept getting harder

The portal was built in 2016, before robotic surgery programs grew into the complex, multi-system operations they are now. It did not fail all at once. It aged. Every year the programs got more instruments, more system models, more cases, and the tool that indexed all of it stayed exactly as simple as the day it shipped. The gap between the job and the software widened until it became the story.

By the time I arrived the numbers were brutal, and they were the starting line, not a scoreboard. Seventy-five percent of users were dissatisfied. Navigation pain rated around eight out of ten, with the core functions buried three and four levels deep. The returns workflow was so ambiguous it produced a roughly seventy percent error rate. People were getting the return wrong more often than they got it right.

Sit with what that means. Nurses were making inventory ordering decisions, decisions that touch surgical readiness, on top of data they could not reliably reach or trust. The failure was not cosmetic. It had quietly become a patient-adjacent risk wearing the costume of a clunky internal tool.

03 // Into the field

I stopped reading the portal and started watching the people

You cannot design your way out of a problem you have only read about in a ticket. So I went to where the work actually happens. Contextual inquiry, in the real OR workflow, not a lab. I watched nurses use the thing under real pressure, in the gaps between cases, and I watched what they did when the software got in the way.

Then I made the observation formal. A moderated usability test plan: 5 to 6 ROCOS, a deliberate mix of novices and experts, sixty-minute sessions over Zoom, eight defined task scenarios spanning navigation, ordering, and utilization interpretation. I wanted to see exactly where each person slowed down, guessed, or gave up.

The same pattern kept surfacing. It was not that nurses could not find the data. The portal had plenty of data. It was that the data never resolved into the thing they actually needed to know.

04 // The insight

They did not need more data. They needed the decision the data was hiding.

Every nurse was doing the same silent math. Scanning columns, holding numbers in her head, trying to convert a spreadsheet of usage into a single answer: do I reorder this, and how much. The software handed her raw material and made her do the reasoning by hand, every time, under time pressure, with a case starting soon.

That was the whole problem in one sentence. The decision was in there, but it was buried under the data instead of expressed by it. Nobody needed another table. They needed to see when and how much to reorder without translating anything.

Once I could name it, the redesign stopped being about tidying screens and became about surfacing a judgment. Show the decision, not the raw material it is made from.

The decision was in there. It was just buried under the data instead of expressed by it.
05 // The bet

I made the riskiest view the primary view

So I made a bet that made people nervous, including me. I moved the treemap to the front and made it the primary ordering view. Cell size encodes instrument quantity and usage, so the reorder signal is not something you calculate. It is something you see. The big cells and the small cells tell you where attention is owed before you have read a single number.

The risk was real, and I will not pretend otherwise. A treemap has a steeper first-use learning curve than a spreadsheet, and these nurses were fluent in spreadsheet-style order history. I was asking experts to give up a familiar tool for an unfamiliar one, and betting that the payoff on day two would outweigh the friction on day one. If I was wrong, I had made an already frustrated group more frustrated.

I was not wrong. In testing, nurses reported understanding their instrument usage at a glance for the first time. Task completion on the ordering scenarios improved significantly over the baseline. The unfamiliar view did not slow them down. It was the first thing that had ever sped them up.

06 // The architecture

I threw out the filing cabinet and organized around the job

The treemap fixed the ordering view. The navigation needed a deeper change. The old portal was organized around data objects: instruments here, systems there, orders and returns in their own separate silos. It was a filing cabinet. It made perfect sense to the database and none to the nurse, who had to context-switch across four silos to complete one real task.

I reorganized the whole thing around what nurses actually do, not what the data is called. Three jobs: check utilization, place orders, resolve exceptions. Everything else served those. Functions that had been buried three and four levels deep now sat one step from the door. The depth did not shrink a little. It collapsed.

Then I made the rest of the tool honest. Returns, the seventy-percent-error workflow, was rebuilt with progressive disclosure and inline validation so the correct path is the obvious path, caught in the moment instead of discovered after the mistake. I added a cost-versus-use scatter plot so administrators can spot over-ordered instruments on sight, and a utilization summary that breaks 533 cases and 1,180 hours across Xi, Si, and SP, with a history calendar for capacity planning. And because OR nurses move between desktop and tablet across a single shift, I designed it responsive, so the tool follows them instead of pinning them to a desk.

07 // From judgment to roadmap

Every decision had to survive PM and engineering, so I made the reasoning visible

A redesign this opinionated only ships if the people building it trust the priorities. So I did not hand over a pile of screens and hope. I built a Value by Feasibility by Priority matrix, mapped every feature to a jobs-to-be-done goal, and scored it. The bets were legible. Anyone could see why the treemap came before the nice-to-haves, and why a given feature earned its place in the build order.

That artifact became the roadmap PM and engineering actually worked from. It is the part of the job most designers treat as someone else's problem. I treat it as where the design either survives contact with reality or quietly dies in a backlog.

08 // What it proves

The honest payoff, and why it counts

I will not hand you an invented before-and-after satisfaction stat, because I do not have one and a fabricated number would insult the real work. Here is what is true. Navigation depth collapsed from three and four levels down to a single step. Nurses understood their instrument usage at a glance for the first time, validated in moderated testing, not asserted in a deck. Task completion on ordering improved measurably over the baseline. The correct return path became the obvious one by design.

None of that came from adding features. It came from a handful of decisions: name the hidden decision, make the risky view primary, organize around the job instead of the database, make the reasoning legible enough to ship. Then I saw those decisions through to a shipped, responsive product, not a handoff.

That is the whole point. The problem I was handed was a patient-adjacent risk dressed up as a clunky portal. The thing I shipped lets a nurse see, in a glance between cases, whether she is about to run short. That is the difference between a screen and a decision.
